## The one deal used in every lesson
**Tamar Group plc** (acquirer, listed) buys **Wychwood Ltd** (target, listed). All figures £m unless per share; tax 25 %.

| | Tamar Group plc | Wychwood Ltd |
|---|---|---|
| Net income | 120 | 40 |
| Shares (m) | 100 | 50 |
| EPS (£) | 1.20 | 0.80 |
| Share price (£) | 18.00 | 8.00 |
| P/E | 15.0× | 10.0× |
| Market cap | 1,800 | 400 |

Offer: **£10.00 per share** = 25 % premium → **equity purchase price £500m** → implied P/E on the offer **12.5×** (500 / 40). Wychwood net debt 0 (kept simple; EV = equity value). Wychwood book net assets 220; fair-value uplift on brand intangibles 60. Tamar's cash earns 4 % pre-tax; new debt costs 6 % pre-tax. Cost synergies 20 pre-tax per year from year 2 (10 in year 1); one-off integration cost 20; Tamar discount rate 8 %.

Derived numbers (checked, used across lessons; `src/lib/finance/merger.ts` must reproduce them):
- All-stock: new shares 500 / 18 = 27.78m; pro-forma NI 160; shares 127.78m; EPS £1.252 → **+4.4 % accretive**.
- All-cash from balance-sheet cash: foregone interest 500 × 4 % = 20 pre-tax → 15 after tax; NI 145; EPS £1.45 → **+20.8 %**.
- All-debt: interest 30 pre-tax → 22.5 after tax; NI 137.5; EPS £1.375 → **+14.6 %**.
- 50 / 50 debt + stock (lesson 3): interest 250 × 6 % = 15 → 11.25 after tax; new shares 250 / 18 = 13.89m; NI 148.75; shares 113.89m; EPS £1.306 → **+8.8 %**; with run-rate synergies 20 pre-tax → 15 after tax: NI 163.75; EPS £1.438 → **+19.8 %**.
- Earnings yields: Tamar 1 / 15 = 6.67 %; Wychwood at the offer price 40 / 500 = 8.0 %; after-tax cost of cash 3.0 %; after-tax cost of debt 4.5 %.
- Goodwill: 500 − (220 + 60) = **220**. Stretch: DTL on the uplift 60 × 25 % = 15 → goodwill 235.
- Premium paid 500 − 400 = **100**. PV of synergies (after tax 15 run-rate from year 2, 7.5 in year 1, perpetuity, 8 %): ≈ 7.5 / 1.08 + (15 / 0.08) / 1.08 ≈ 6.9 + 173.6 = **180.5**, less integration cost 20 → ≈ 160 > premium 100.

## Lesson 1 — `why-companies-acquire` · "Why do companies buy other companies — and tell me about a deal"
(a) **Interview questions:** "Why do companies make acquisitions?" (`sa-core`); "Tell me about a deal you've been following" (`sa-core`, Tier C in every UK round).
(b) **Worked numbers:** the Tamar / Wychwood headline: £500m for a £400m company = 25 % premium; 12.5× earnings vs Tamar's own 15×; rationale = Wychwood's distribution network lets Tamar cut £20m of duplicated cost.
(c) **Trap:** **"A company acquires because it will be bigger and bigger is better."** Size is not a rationale; the buyer must expect the target to be worth more *inside* the buyer than the price paid — synergies, capabilities, market access, or a cheaper way to grow than building.
(d) **Canonical answer (≈ 75 words):** Companies acquire when they believe a target is worth more in their hands than the price they pay. The usual reasons are cost synergies from removing duplicated functions, revenue synergies from cross-selling or new markets, buying a capability or technology faster than building it, consolidating a fragmented market, or using cheap financing to buy earnings. A good answer names the rationale *and* the risk: paying a premium only makes sense if the synergies are real and deliverable.
(e) **Predict gate:** "Tamar pays 25 % more than Wychwood's market price. Who gains that £100m on day one?" — options: Tamar's shareholders / **Wychwood's shareholders** / nobody, it nets out. Explain: the premium is a transfer to the seller; the buyer only wins if PV(synergies) exceeds it.
(f) **Widget:** none in this lesson (the `template` block `deal_summary` is the interactive piece). Prompts: fill every field for Tamar / Wychwood; then fill it for a real deal from this month's Pulse.
(g) **Your turn:** "Kestrel Foods plc, P/E 18, offers £1.2bn for a rival with net income £80m. Give the implied P/E, the premium if the rival's market cap is £1.0bn, and two plausible rationales." Rubric: implied P/E 15× · premium 20 % / £200m · one cost and one revenue rationale · notes the premium must be covered by synergies.
(h) **Quick-fire:** Q: Name two cost synergies → A: shared head office, combined procurement. Q: Which is more credible, cost or revenue synergies? → A: cost — the buyer controls them. Q: What is a control premium? → A: the extra paid over market price to buy 100 % and run the company. Q: Strategic vs financial buyer — who usually pays more? → A: strategic, because of synergies.
(i) **Lens — TMT:** *"Acquihires and platform deals"* — In technology the rationale is often talent or a product wedge rather than earnings: an acquihire buys a team, a platform deal buys distribution or data. Payment is often mostly in stock because growth companies have high-multiple paper and little spare cash. Regulators now scrutinise platform acquisitions of small rivals, so "will it clear?" is part of the rationale. example_q: "Why would a loss-making software company be worth £300m to a larger platform?" — answer outline: capability / product wedge, distribution multiplies the target's revenue, price justified on the acquirer's economics not the target's standalone earnings.
   **Lens — Healthcare:** *"Buying a pipeline, not profits"* — Large pharma acquire biotechs to refill a pipeline before a patent cliff; the target may have no revenue for years, so EPS accretion is irrelevant and the rationale is risk-adjusted NPV of the drugs. Consideration often includes a **contingent value right** (CVR): extra cash if a trial or approval milestone is hit, bridging the valuation gap. example_q: "Why is a CVR common in biotech M&A and rare elsewhere?" — outline: binary outcomes, information asymmetry, lets the seller share upside without the buyer paying for unproven success.
(j) **Follow-up ladder:** why → "which reason applies to the Tamar deal?" → "what could make the deal destroy value?" → "would you have advised Tamar to pay £10?" → "how was it financed and what does that tell you?"
**Sources (structure):** M&I "Investment banking interview questions" (M&A rationale category); WSO "20 most asked, London edition" (deal discussion expectation); financefluency Evercore firm page (deal question with "would you have advised it").

## Lesson 2 — `accretion-dilution-concepts` · "Is the deal accretive or dilutive — and why?"
(a) **Interview questions:** "What is accretion / dilution?" (`sa-core`); "Tamar at 15× buys Wychwood at 12.5× in an all-stock deal — accretive or dilutive?" (`sa-core`); "Cash, stock or debt — which is most accretive and why?" (`sa-core`).
(b) **Worked numbers:** all-stock case: 27.78m new shares; NI 160 over 127.78m shares = £1.252 vs £1.20 → +4.4 %. Earnings-yield framing: Tamar "pays" 6.67 % (its own earnings yield) for something earning 8.0 % on the price paid → accretive. Cash: cost 3.0 % after tax → +20.8 %. Debt: cost 4.5 % → +14.6 %.
(c) **Trap:** **"The target has a lower P/E than us, so the deal is accretive."** The comparison is against the P/E *at the offer price* (12.5×, not 10×) and against the after-tax cost of the funding actually used; a lower-P/E target bought with expensive debt can still dilute.
(d) **Canonical answer (≈ 85 words):** A deal is accretive if the buyer's pro-forma EPS is higher than its standalone EPS, dilutive if lower. The shortcut: compare the target's earnings yield at the offer price with the after-tax cost of the funding. For stock, that cost is the buyer's own earnings yield — the inverse of its P/E — so a higher-P/E buyer using stock to buy a lower-P/E target is accretive. Cash and debt are cheaper still at today's rates, so all-cash deals are usually the most accretive; accretion is not the same as value creation.
(e) **Predict gate:** "Tamar (15×) pays 12.5× in all stock. Pro-forma EPS goes…" — options: **up** / down / unchanged. Explain with the earnings-yield comparison 8.0 % > 6.67 %.
(f) **Widget `accretion_rule`:** sliders acquirer P/E, offer P/E, % stock, pre-tax cost of debt, tax; outputs pro-forma EPS vs standalone, accretion %, and a "cost of currency" bar (stock yield, after-tax debt, after-tax cash). Prompts: "Slide the offer P/E until the all-stock deal turns dilutive — what number is it?" (answer: 15×); "Set 100 % debt and raise the rate until the deal is dilutive" (8 % pre-tax → 6 % after tax → still accretive at 8 % yield… raise to 10.7 % pre-tax); "Why does the cash line barely move when you change the acquirer's P/E?"
(g) **Your turn:** "Buyer P/E 12, offer P/E 14, all-stock. Accretive or dilutive, and by roughly how much if buyer NI £60m on 50m shares and target NI £20m?" Rubric: dilutive · buyer yield 8.3 % < target yield 7.1 % · new shares 280/14.4 = 19.4m · EPS 80/69.4 = £1.15 vs £1.20 ≈ −4 % · mentions synergies could reverse it.
(h) **Quick-fire:** Q: Cost of stock as a currency? → A: the acquirer's earnings yield (1 / P/E). Q: Cost of cash? → A: after-tax interest foregone. Q: Why is cash usually most accretive? → A: after-tax interest rates are below most earnings yields. Q: Does accretion mean the deal creates value? → A: no — it is an EPS arithmetic outcome, not an NPV.
(i) **Lens — TMT:** *"High-multiple paper"* — A software acquirer on 40× earnings has a 2.5 % earnings yield, so almost any target is "accretive" in stock — which is why serial tech acquirers pay in shares and why analysts discount EPS accretion in the sector; they look at revenue growth and cash-flow per share instead. Loss-making targets have no P/E at all, so the rule collapses and the conversation moves to EV/Revenue and strategic fit. example_q: "Why do investors ignore EPS accretion for a high-growth software acquirer?" — outline: tiny earnings yield makes accretion trivial, dilution of ownership still real, focus on growth durability.
   **Lens — Healthcare:** *"Dilutive by design"* — Pharma buying a pre-revenue biotech is dilutive for years: no target earnings, extra R&D expense, often financed with debt. Boards accept it because the value is in future risk-adjusted cash flows; the market judges the deal on probability of success and peak sales. Expect "dilutive until launch in year 4, accretive after" language in announcements. example_q: "A £2bn biotech deal is guided as dilutive for four years — is that a bad deal?" — outline: not necessarily; EPS ignores pipeline value; check rNPV vs price, and financing cost.
(j) **Ladder:** define → rule of thumb → "which currency and why?" → "what if rates rise 2 points?" → "is accretive always good?"
**Sources:** WSP "M&A interview questions"; M&I merger-model questions; WSO real-question threads (P/E 15 vs 10 all-stock).

## Lesson 3 — `accretion-dilution-calculations` · "Walk me through the merger maths"
(a) **Interview question:** "Company A buys Company B with 50 % debt and 50 % stock — walk me through pro-forma EPS" (`sa-core` for the method, `sa-stretch` for doing it with synergies and fees under time pressure).
(b) **Worked numbers:** purchase price 500 = 250 debt + 250 stock. Interest 250 × 6 % = 15 pre-tax, 11.25 after tax. New shares 250 / 18 = 13.89m. Pro-forma NI = 120 + 40 − 11.25 = 148.75. Shares 113.89m. EPS £1.306 vs £1.20 → +8.8 %. Add synergies 20 pre-tax → 15 after tax → NI 163.75 → EPS £1.438 → +19.8 %. (Fees and new D&A from write-ups are named as further deductions — stretch.)
(c) **Trap:** **"Add the two net incomes and divide by the acquirer's shares."** You must also (1) deduct the after-tax cost of the funding and (2) add the shares issued; forgetting either is the most common failure.
(d) **Canonical answer (≈ 80 words):** Start with combined net income. Subtract the after-tax interest on any new debt and the after-tax interest lost on any cash used. Add after-tax synergies and subtract any new amortisation from write-ups. That gives pro-forma net income. Then take the acquirer's shares and add the new shares issued — purchase price paid in stock divided by the acquirer's share price. Divide the two, compare with standalone EPS, and state the percentage accretion or dilution.
(e) **Predict gate:** "Switching the deal from 50 / 50 to all-stock makes accretion…" — options: higher / **lower** / the same. Explain: stock costs 6.67 % vs debt 4.5 %.
(f) **Widget:** `accretion_rule` in "full mode" (exposes cash / debt / stock split, synergies, fees). Prompts: "Move the split to 100 % debt — why is EPS higher but the balance sheet riskier?"; "Add £20m synergies — by how much does accretion change?" (≈ +11 points); "Find the synergies needed for an all-stock deal at 16× to break even."
(g) **Your turn (`fill_numbers` in the lesson):** the same structure with buyer NI 90 / 60m shares / price £15, target NI 30, price 360, 40 % debt at 5 %, 60 % stock, tax 25 %. Rubric: interest 7.2 → 5.4 after tax · new shares 14.4m · NI 114.6 · shares 74.4m · EPS £1.54 vs £1.50 → +2.7 %.
(h) **Quick-fire:** Q: New shares issued = ? → A: stock consideration ÷ acquirer share price. Q: After-tax interest = ? → A: debt × rate × (1 − tax). Q: Where do synergies go? → A: after-tax, into pro-forma net income. Q: What else reduces pro-forma NI? → A: amortisation of written-up intangibles and lost interest on cash.
(i) **Lens — TMT:** *"Share-based comp and dilution stacking"* — In tech, existing employee options and RSUs already dilute; a stock deal adds another layer. Analysts model diluted share counts (treasury-stock method, chapter 14) on both sides and often present "cash EPS" excluding amortisation of acquired intangibles, which is large in software deals. example_q: "Why does a software acquirer report 'non-GAAP' EPS after a deal?" — outline: acquired-intangible amortisation is a big non-cash charge, management wants like-for-like, be sceptical.
   **Lens — Healthcare:** *"Earn-outs in the maths"* — With a CVR or earn-out, the purchase price is uncertain; the model runs the base price and a "milestone hit" case where extra consideration is paid later, often funded with debt, so accretion is a range. In-process R&D from the target is expensed or amortised depending on the standard, hitting EPS with no cash effect. example_q: "How would you show a £200m CVR in an accretion model?" — outline: two scenarios, later-dated cash outflow, interest cost only when paid, note probability.
(j) **Ladder:** walk → "what did you forget?" (fees / write-ups) → "which line is most uncertain?" (synergies) → "what leverage does the debt imply?" → "would you finance it differently?"
**Sources:** WSP merger-model tutorial (structure of the pro-forma bridge); M&I merger-model questions; CFI accretion / dilution explainer.

## Lesson 4 — `synergies-and-deal-structure` · "Synergies, goodwill and how the deal is put together"
(a) **Interview questions:** "What are synergies and which are more credible?" (`sa-core`); "What is goodwill and how is it created?" (`sa-core`); "What deal protections might a buyer or seller ask for?" (`sa-stretch`).
(b) **Worked numbers:** premium paid 100. Cost synergies 20 pre-tax (10 in year 1) → 15 after tax → PV ≈ 180 less integration 20 = 160 > 100 premium. Goodwill: price 500 − fair-value net assets (220 + 60) = 220. Stretch: DTL 15 on the write-up → goodwill 235.
(c) **Trap:** **"Goodwill is the premium over the share price."** It is the excess of price over the *fair value of identifiable net assets*; the market-price premium and goodwill are different numbers (100 vs 220 here) because the balance sheet was worth less than the market cap.
(d) **Canonical answer (≈ 80 words):** Synergies are the extra profit the combined company earns that neither could alone: cost synergies from removing duplicated spend, revenue synergies from cross-selling. Cost synergies are more credible because the buyer controls them. Goodwill is the accounting plug: purchase price less the fair value of the target's identifiable net assets after writing assets up to fair value; it sits on the balance sheet and is tested for impairment rather than amortised. A deal is worth doing when the present value of synergies exceeds the premium.
(e) **Predict gate:** "Tamar writes Wychwood's brand up by £60m. Goodwill goes…" — options: up / **down** / unchanged. Explain: goodwill is the residual after identifiable assets.
(f) **Widget `synergy_npv`:** sliders run-rate synergies, phase-in years, one-off cost, discount rate; output NPV bar vs premium bar. Prompts: "At what run-rate do synergies just cover the £100m premium?" (≈ 9–10 pre-tax); "Double the phase-in to four years — how much NPV is lost?"; "Set the discount rate to 12 % — is the deal still justified?" Stretch widget `ppa_goodwill`: price, write-ups, DTL toggle → goodwill plug and the new D&A / DTL lines.
(g) **Your turn:** "A buyer pays £900m for a company with book net assets £500m; PP&E is written up by £100m and a customer list of £50m is recognised. Compute goodwill, then explain what happens if the target underperforms." Rubric: fair-value net assets 650 · goodwill 250 · impairment charge, non-cash, hits net income not cash · write-ups create extra depreciation / amortisation.
(h) **Quick-fire:** Q: Is goodwill amortised under IFRS? → A: no, impairment-tested annually. Q: One-off vs run-rate synergies? → A: run-rate is the steady annual level once fully achieved. Q: What is a break fee? → A: a payment if one side walks away — protects the other's costs. Q: Why do sellers like an earn-out less than cash? → A: they carry delivery risk and the buyer controls the business.
(i) **Lens — TMT:** *"Revenue synergies the market won't pay for"* — Tech deals are often sold on cross-selling into a larger installed base. Investors haircut revenue synergies heavily because they depend on customers' choices; cost synergies in R&D and sales are what moves the share price. Intangibles (developed technology, customer relationships) dominate the write-ups, so amortisation is large. example_q: "A deal announcement promises £100m of revenue synergies — how would you treat that?" — outline: haircut 50 %+, check retention assumptions, focus on cost synergies and dilution.
   **Lens — Healthcare:** *"IPR&D and the roll-up"* — In a biotech acquisition, most of the price is allocated to in-process R&D — the pipeline — which is not amortised until the drug is approved; if a trial fails it is written off. Healthcare services deals (dental, veterinary, care homes) are roll-ups where synergies are procurement, back office and multiple arbitrage — buying small at 6× and being valued at 12×. example_q: "Where does the purchase price go on the balance sheet in a biotech deal?" — outline: mostly IPR&D intangible, some goodwill, impairment on trial failure.
(j) **Ladder:** synergies → "how would you check management's number?" → goodwill → "what does an impairment tell you?" → "which structure protects Tamar if Wychwood misses its plan?"
**Sources:** WSP "M&A interview questions" (goodwill / synergies section); M&I PPA explainer (structure); FT Lexicon-style definitions of break fees / earn-outs.

## Cheat sheet (`content/cheatsheets/ma.json`)
- **Formulas:** accretion % = pro-forma EPS / standalone EPS − 1 · new shares = stock consideration / acquirer price · after-tax cost of debt = r × (1 − t) · cost of stock = 1 / P/E · goodwill = price − fair-value identifiable net assets · deal test: PV(synergies) > premium.
- **Canonical:** why acquire · accretion / dilution rule · merger maths walk · synergies and goodwill (the four answers above).
- **Traps:** market P/E vs offer P/E · forgetting new shares · forgetting after-tax funding cost · goodwill ≠ premium · accretion ≠ value.
- **One-liners:** "Accretive if the target's earnings yield at the price paid beats the after-tax cost of what you pay with."
- **You may hear (ft-only):** full three-statement merger model, asset vs stock sale and tax elections, purchase-accounting DTL mechanics in detail, exchange-ratio collars.
