# A realistic first-year application timeline

## August to September: set-up

Before term starts, get the boring things done. Write a one-page CV with three bullet points per role that each start with a verb and end with a number. Make a spreadsheet with every firm you might apply to, the programme name, the opening date and the deadline. Most spring week applications open in late August and a surprising number close before the end of October, so the spreadsheet is the whole game.

Set up a professional email signature and a LinkedIn profile that says what year you are and what you are interested in. It does not need to be impressive; it needs to exist so that people you message can see you are a real student.

## October: the first wave

Submit the applications for the firms you care about most in the first two weeks of October. Rolling review is real: the reader who sees your application in week one has a fuller quota than the reader in week six. Use the same core story about why banking across every application and change only the firm-specific paragraph.

Start doing two numerical practice tests a week. Do not leave the online tests until you receive one; some firms send the test within an hour of your application and give you three days.

## November to December: interviews and holding your nerve

Video interviews land from November. The questions are predictable: why banking, why this firm, a time you worked in a team, a deal in the news. Record yourself answering each once, watch it back, and fix the two worst habits. Most people talk too fast and never say a number.

If you have no interviews by mid-December, that is normal. Keep applying to the second-wave firms and keep networking. Rejections in this period say more about volume than about you.

## January to March: second wave and conversion

Some firms run a January cycle. Apply to those and to any insight days that appear. When a spring week offer arrives, the work shifts from getting in to converting: read about each division, prepare one question per session, and find two people who did the same programme last year to ask what the assessment looked like.

## A worked example of the numbers

Suppose you apply to fifteen firms. A typical outcome is ten rejections at the application stage, three online tests that go nowhere, and two interviews. One offer from two interviews is a good hit rate. If the funnel looks worse than that at the application stage, the CV needs work; if it collapses at interview, the stories need work. Diagnose which one before changing anything.
