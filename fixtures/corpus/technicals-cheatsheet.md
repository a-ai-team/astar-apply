# Technicals cheat sheet for a second year with one finance module

## The three statements in one breath

The income statement tells you what the business earned over a period. The cash flow statement tells you what actually moved in and out of the bank account. The balance sheet is the photograph at the end. Net income links the first to the third through retained earnings, and it starts the cash flow statement. If you can say that sentence calmly you are already ahead.

## The depreciation walk, with a number

Depreciation goes up by 10. Pre-tax profit falls 10, tax falls 2.5 at a 25 percent rate, net income falls 7.5. On the cash flow statement, start at minus 7.5, add back 10 of non-cash depreciation, cash is up 2.5. On the balance sheet, cash is up 2.5 and fixed assets are down 10, so assets are down 7.5; retained earnings are down 7.5. It balances. The reason cash goes up is purely the tax shield.

## Equity value to enterprise value

$$EV = \text{Equity value} + \text{Net debt} + \text{Minority interest} + \text{Preferred}$$

Worked number: equity value 500 million pounds, debt 200, cash 80, no minorities or preferred. Net debt is 120 and enterprise value is 620 million. Multiples that sit above the interest line, like price to earnings, use equity value; multiples that sit above interest, like enterprise value to EBITDA, use enterprise value. The rule is to match the numerator to whoever the denominator belongs to.

## Discounted cash flow in five sentences

Forecast unlevered free cash flow for five years. Discount each year at the weighted average cost of capital. Add a terminal value, usually from a perpetuity growth rate of two to three percent or an exit multiple. Sum the present values to get enterprise value. Subtract net debt to reach equity value and divide by shares for a price.

Worked number: cash flows of 50 million a year for five years at a 10 percent discount rate are worth about 190 million; a terminal value of 50 times 1.02 divided by 0.08, discounted back five years, adds roughly 396 million; enterprise value is around 586 million.

## Why the discount rate matters more than the forecast

Moving the discount rate from 10 to 9 percent on the example above adds about 60 million of value, which is more than a whole year of cash flow. Interviewers love asking what happens to the valuation when you change one input, so know the direction and the rough size for the discount rate, the growth rate and the exit multiple.

## The LBO in one paragraph

A sponsor buys a business with a lot of debt, uses the company's own cash flows to pay that debt down, and sells it after about five years. Returns come from debt paydown, earnings growth and any change in the exit multiple. Worked number: buy at 400 million with 240 of debt and 160 of equity; exit at 560 million with 120 of debt left, so equity is 440 million, which is 2.75 times the money in.

## Three traps

One: saying that EBITDA is cash. It is not; working capital and capex are missing. Two: adding cash to enterprise value instead of subtracting it. Three: forgetting to say that the balance sheet balances at the end of a walk. Interviewers wait for that sentence.
